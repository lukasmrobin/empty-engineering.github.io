Chapter 1Paving the Way

Three fields, six feminisms, research methods, intersectionality.

Feminist psych / Psych of women / Psych of gender
Ties to women's studies + activism / women's lives and experiences / social + biological processes creating male-female differences.
Androcentric
Male-centered; the flaw early psychology was criticized for.
First wave
Seneca Falls Declaration 1848; vote won 1920s; then lost momentum.
Second wave
1960s. AWP formed 1969; APA Division 35 (Psychology of Women, now Society for the Psychology of Women) and CWP both 1973.
Third wave
Activism: reproductive freedom, ending sexualized violence, women in leadership. (Your review sheet also lists a fourth wave, empowerment; the slides don't define it, so check your textbook.)
Mary Calkins (1863-1930)
Denied Harvard PhD because she was a woman; built a lab at Wellesley; first female president of APA and of the American Philosophical Association.
Liberal
Full legal and social equality via changes in laws, customs, values.
Radical
Male control/domination is the most fundamental oppression; drives research on violence against women. Separatism: escape patriarchy through woman-only communities.
Womanism (women-of-color)
Poverty, racism, jobs, health care, education; rejects separatism.
Cultural
Stresses male-female differences; useful for valuing women's unpaid work.
Global / Transnational
Global: feminism as worldwide movement, links to neo-colonialism and global capitalism. Transnational: gender oppression together with colonialism, capitalism, exploitation.
hooks (1984)
Feminism = movement to end sexism and sexist oppression. Feminist = believes women are valuable and change is needed. Profeminist = man who acknowledges women's leadership.
Quantitative
Measure, average over a group, compare with statistics. Survey = beliefs/attitudes. Correlational = relationship, not causation. Longitudinal = 2+ time points. Archival = existing data. Experiment = manipulate variables, shows causation.
Qualitative
Open-ended, no systematic counting. Discourse analysis (talk), ethnography (live in community), case study (one person).
Bias in 5 stages
Question formulation, designing (participants), data analysis (differences over similarities), interpreting (overgeneralizing), publication bias (differences get published).
Feminist research values
Empirical research is worthwhile; methods must be critiqued; women and men can both do it; science is never fully value-neutral; social/historical/political forces shape behavior.
Intersectionality
Everyone belongs to many groups at once. Ask: Who is included in a category? What role does inequality play? Where are the similarities?
Book themes
Women lack full equality; women and men are both different and similar; psychology can promote social change.

Self-check

Q: Which research method can show causation?
Experiments. Correlational studies cannot.

Q: Why was Calkins significant?
Elected APA president yet denied a Harvard PhD: example of historical marginalization.

Chapter 2Gender, Status, and Power

Gender as a system of power, performed in everyday interaction.

Sex vs gender (Unger)
Sex = biology (genes, anatomy). Gender = traits a society deems appropriate for each sex. Current view: gender is a classification system affecting access to power and resources.
Power vs status
Power = control others' outcomes via resources. Status = social standing that earns respect.
Patriarchy
System allocating more power and status to men (laws, religion, public discourse, wealth, leisure, education).
Legitimizing myths
Beliefs that justify hierarchy by emphasizing differences.
Prejudice / Discrimination / Sexism / Heterosexism / Oppression
Prejudice = negative attitude. Discrimination = differential treatment. Sexism = prejudice + discrimination by sex/gender. Heterosexism = negative attitudes toward LGBT people. Oppression = widespread, systematic prejudice and discrimination against a non-dominant group.
Gender construction through female bodies
Most gender construction in industrialized cultures happens through women's appearance.
Self-presentation / Self-fulfilling prophecy
Acting out a self for others' expectations / expectations make themselves come true (Rosenthal and Jacobson, 1968).
Doing gender
Gender as social performance, usually not conscious; women's second-class status is maintained through everyday interaction. Femininity only has meaning in contrast to masculinity.
Interaction tactics
Talking down, ordering, silencing; intrusive interruptions; conversational dominance (masculine style). Nonverbal: high status takes more space, touches freely; visual dominance = look at subordinates while speaking, away when they speak.
Microaggressions
Brief verbal/behavioral indignities signaling hostility toward a devalued group.
Backlash
Negative reaction against women who violate gender norms.
Double bind
Subordinate is criticized for acting like the dominant group (assertive woman = competent but not likable) and for acting like a model subordinate (not competent). No-win.
Gender management (Rudman and Glick)
Softening a woman's impact, showing niceness plus some competence.
Gender typing
Taking social gender distinctions into the self-concept.
Denial of personal discrimination (Crosby)
People admit discrimination exists but not that it happened to them. Entitlement: women justify lower pay, compare with other underpaid women.
Ambivalent sexism (Glick and Fiske)
Hostile: women inferior and threaten to take men's place. Benevolent: women are special, to be cherished and protected. Ambivalent sexists hold both.
SDO (Sidanius and Pratto)
Support for dominance of 'superior' over 'inferior' groups; relevant to race, religion, sexuality, gender, nationality.
Making a difference
Individual: pro-feminist community, feminist therapy, women's/gender studies. Interpersonal: confront prejudice, disrupt norms. Structural: collective action.

Self-check

Q: Hostile vs benevolent sexism?
Hostile = women threaten men's rightful place; benevolent = women are cherished and protected. Both are sexism.

Q: What is a double bind?
Criticized for acting like the dominant group and for not acting like it.

Chapter 3Images of Women

Language, media, and stereotypes shape and distort how women are seen.

Linguistic sexism
Inequitable treatment of women and men built into everyday language.
Gender-marked terms
Male is the norm, female the variation ('basketball' vs 'women's basketball'); trivializes women. Also 'people = male' bias and 'animal = male' bias.
Slang
Women's slang is sexual, objectifying, dehumanizing (animals, body parts); similar terms for men are far rarer.
Media underrepresentation
Women ~51% of population but ~30% of speaking roles in top 100 movies since 2007; underrepresented in prime-time commercials except health/beauty, and in comics.
Face-ism (Archer et al.)
Proportion of image devoted to the face; higher for men than women, European Americans over African Americans; Black women lowest (Zuckerman and Kieffer).
Sexual objectification
Reducing women to body parts; more female than male nudity in every culture studied. Idealized beauty (bigger breasts, thinner arms, smaller waist); digital alteration.
Body image / Social comparison
Mental picture plus feelings about body. Idealized images make women feel worse by comparison; Western media globalizes dissatisfaction; research mostly on white college women.
Self-objectification
Internalizing an observer's view of your body as primary; self-surveillance splits self into subject and object, drains cognitive resources.
Objectification theory (Fredrickson and Roberts, 1997)
In a sexually objectifying culture, girls and women internalize an observer's perspective.
Invisible women
Women of color (White women overrepresented), older women (invisibility rises with age), bigger women (tokens), lower-income women (Black women overrepresented in welfare discourse), women as authorities (focus stays on looks/family).
Stereotype / Schema
Stereotype = 'theory' about how a group thinks, looks, behaves. Schema = mental framework guiding experience.
Content of stereotypes
Physical: women dainty, men strong. Traits: women communal/affective, men agentic/instrumental (Broverman 1972). Roles: housewife-mother, sexy, athletic, career woman / blue-collar, athletic, macho, businessman.
Occupational stereotypes
Most jobs gender-typed (Beggs and Doolittle 1975/1993); children learn them by 6-8.
Sexuality, race, class stereotypes
Lesbians 'butch', gay men effeminate. 'Typical' woman = white and middle-class. Black women: Mammy (nurturing, asexual), Jezebel (promiscuous), Sapphire (domineering, aggressive).
Are stereotypes accurate?
Some kernel of truth (Jussim); useful shortcuts; hard to change; shape what information is taken in.
Impacts
Gender schema: believing stereotypes express your identity. Stereotype threat: fear of confirming a negative stereotype impairs performance. Self-fulfilling prophecy; prescriptive stereotypes say how people should be; powerful people stereotype less powerful; can prime harassment.
Making a difference
Feminist language reform (Pauwels); informed consumers using voices and dollars; Jean Kilbourne.

Self-check

Q: Descriptive vs prescriptive stereotype?
Descriptive = how people are believed to be; prescriptive = how they should be.

Q: What does face-ism measure?
Proportion of an image given to the face.

Chapter 4The Meanings of Difference

How difference is measured, and what we do with it: math and emotion.

Similarities vs differences tradition
Similarities: women and men alike in intelligence, personality, abilities, goals. Differences: fundamental differences to recognize and honor.
Statistics terms
Mean difference = average gap. Variability = spread. Statistically significant = unlikely by chance. Confounding = variables mixed so cause is unclear.
Meta-analysis / Moderator variable
Quantitative summary of many studies (Hedges and Becker) / a variable that changes another variable's effect. (Effect size = how big the difference is.)
Historical bias
Late-1800s research assumed women's inferiority and sought biological causes (smaller brains).
Variability hypothesis
Men more variable: similar averages but more men at the extremes. Early women psychologists worked to dispute inferiority claims.
Math
Boys higher only on advanced math; girls get better math grades overall (quiet, neat, effort, drive for mastery). Factors: biology, math as male domain, classroom bias, stereotype threat.
Female underprediction effect
Women score lower on standardized tests but earn higher college grades, so tests underpredict them.
Emotion stereotypes
Women = 'the emotional sex' (depends on narrow definition of emotion; men's displays often not labeled emotionality). Women have greater decoding ability.
Culture and emotion
Display rules = who may express what, when, how. Independent vs interdependent self. Gender differences smaller in collectivist cultures.
Feeling rules (Shields)
What it means to experience an emotion and what others expect you to feel. Parents talk about emotions more with daughters.
Emotion, status, power
Same emotion perceived differently by status; right to show anger is power.
Relationship conflict
Men learn anger controls others; men's inexpressiveness of positive feelings preserves power; women expected to be emotion managers and keep relationships smooth.
Making a difference
Individual: more variability within than between sexes. Interactional: same display judged differently; difference does not justify discrimination. Sociocultural: STEM programs.
Reconciling the traditions
Similarities = equity focus; differences = women's connectedness is a strength, not a weakness.

Self-check

Q: Why is meta-analysis useful?
It summarizes results across many studies instead of relying on one.

Q: What is the female underprediction effect?
Lower test scores but higher grades for women.

Chapter 5Sex, Gender, and Bodies

Sex is less binary than assumed; both sex and gender are socially shaped.

Assumptions challenged
Only two sexes; bodies fall in two natural categories; sex exists independent of beliefs; sex and gender naturally go together.
Sexual differentiation
Gradual genetic, hormonal, anatomical development before birth. 22 pairs of autosomes + sex chromosomes (XX female, XY male).
SRY and hormones
SRY on Y triggers gonads to become testes (~week 6). Androgens: testosterone (internal male structures), DHT (penis, testicle growth). MIH blocks female internal organs. Without this: ovaries, estrogens; vagina, labia, clitoris form largely before ovaries.
Assigned sex
Label from the baby's genital appearance.
Intersex conditions
Klinefelter: XXY. CAIS: androgens fail to prompt male structures; raised female, under 1 in 100,000. Turner: missing sex chromosome, no complete internal structures, can still have female identity. PAIS: ambiguous genitals (XY, little androgen). CAH: enzyme malfunction (cortisol); overproduced hormones act as androgens. 'Hermaphrodite' = outdated label.
Core gender identity
Basic sense of belonging to a sex. In intersex children, largely but not entirely social.
Transgender / Gender dysphoria
Umbrella for gender-variant identities. GD = psychiatric category for disjunction between assigned sex and identity. No evidence cross-gender identity itself causes distress; stigma does. Diagnosis controversial. Gender affirmation surgery.
Sexual orientation
Multidimensional: attraction, affection, behavior, fantasies, attachment. Runs in families (genes or environment, can't tell which). Twins: 66% MZ vs 30% DZ co-twins of gay men (Whitam); little/no genetic link for lesbian/bisexual women. X chromosome linkages in men.
Social construction of sex
Commonsense beliefs about sex are cultural, not universal. Doctors choose 'optimal' gender using reproductive potential, function, appearance, identity.
Third sexes
Hijras (India), fa'afafine (Samoa), berdache/two-spirit (N. American Indian), pledged virgins (Balkans; women who take the men's role). Not considered gay.
Genderqueer / Genderfluid
Outside the binary (non-binary, agender, gender-expansive) / moves between genders over time.
Making a difference
Transphobia/genderism threatens safety; APA non-discrimination policy (2009); accept biological and social diversity.

Self-check

Q: Which gene starts testes development?
SRY on the Y chromosome.

Q: Does GD show cross-gender identity causes distress?
No; stigma creates the problems.

Chapter 6Gendered Identities: Childhood and Adolescence

Theories of gender development, daily influences, adolescent vulnerabilities.

Social learning (social cognitive)
Reinforcement, imitation, observational learning (Mischel; Bussey and Bandura). Implies gender typing can be reduced.
Cognitive developmental (Kohlberg)
Fixed stages; gender constancy = gender is permanent.
Gender schema (Bem)
Early-learned schema guides gender typing. Gender-schematic = highly typed, rely on it; aschematic = less typed.
Parents
Pregnancy question: boy or girl? Son preference extreme enough to cut girls' survival: female infanticide, female-selective abortion; tied to patriarchy. Mothers' talk supportive, emotion-focused; fathers' directive, informative. Girls: pretend play; boys: rough-and-tumble. But parents treat sons and daughters similarly more often than differently.
Peers
Gender segregation; play styles diverge. Boys: competition, risk. Girls: negotiation, cooperation. Relational aggression = damaging relationships/social standing (Crick and Rose).
Environments and media
Color-coded toys, stereotyped marketing; males dominate as main characters; video games most popular and most sexist; sexualization = imposing sexuality on a person.
Intersectionality
Ethnic/cultural heritage and social class shape gender socialization.
Poverty
1 in 5 US children; more violence exposure. Boys act out and get noticed; girls' problems go unnoticed.
Puberty / Menarche
Puberty = physiological change toward reproduction; adolescence = social period. Growth spurt, secondary sex characteristics, weight preoccupation. Menarche: mixed messages, often shame/secrecy. Gender intensification = pressure to conform from early adolescence.
Self-silencing / Relational authenticity
Girls stifle thoughts to be 'nice' / congruence between what a girl feels and does. Self-esteem = overall self-regard.
Harassment (AAUW 2001)
Grades 8-11: 83% of girls, 79% of boys harassed. Boys found it flattering; girls frightened, embarrassed. Often in view of adults.
Making a difference
Media literacy, dance, spiritual values, sports (better grades, less depression), arts; resist gender typing.

Self-check

Q: Gender schematic vs aschematic?
Schematic = rely heavily on gender schema; aschematic = rely more on other schemas.

Q: What is gender intensification?
Increased pressure to conform to gender roles in early adolescence.

Chapter 7Sexuality, Love, and Romance

Cultural scripts shape women's sexual experience, identity, and safety.

Sexual scripts
Who/what/when/where/why of permitted sexual behavior. Romantic ideology can justify stalking, assault, murder. Hookup scripts. Scripts vary by culture, race, class.
Adolescent sexuality
More teen sex outside marriage (bigger rise for girls), earlier first intercourse. Strongest predictor: perceived activity of best friends. Parental caring delayed sex more than parental control (Longmore). Students overestimate peers' risk-taking.
Safer sex and STIs
Scripts leave little room for women to negotiate condoms. Over 75 million with HIV/AIDS, almost half women; US women 23% of cases.
First intercourse (Sprecher)
Women: more guilt, less pleasure (2.95 out of 7). Orgasm experience similar for women and men. Women less likely to report masturbation, not more negative attitudes (Dodson encouraged it).
Lesbian/bisexual identity
Orientation includes love, intimacy, fantasies, behavior, subjective identity. Views: personal fulfillment, falling in love with a person, 'born that way', radical-feminist choice, sin/weakness. Bi stereotyped as phase, for men's enjoyment, promiscuity. Coming out; higher risk of low self-esteem, isolation. Sexual fluidity = change over time.
Intersections
Latina lesbians more likely to stay closeted; Black lesbians face racism, sexism, heterosexism; Asian Americans fear family conflict, taboo on discussing sex.
Hookups and online dating
60-80% of college students have hooked up, but most prefer committed relationships. Men more satisfied. Internet dating: many partners, looser gender restrictions, but easier deception. Script: man's right to initiate, his pleasure first.
Dysfunction and objectification theory
Women: more anxiety, guilt, less satisfaction, suppress desire; objectification leads to self-objectification and alienation from body (Fredrickson and Roberts).
Cultural variations
African American women stereotyped promiscuous. Hispanic: machismo vs marianismo; virginity important. Asian cultures see sexuality as healthy yet Asian American women more conservative. No 'right' way.
Attractiveness and disability
Attractiveness matters (maybe more for hookups; stated vs actual choices differ). Hostile sexism and SDO predict men's preference for attractive partners. Women with disabilities: stereotyped as asexual, needing caretakers, heterosexual, grateful for any man.
Sex talk
Women's terms mostly negative; men's positive. Intercourse slang implies something done to women. Metaphors: food, sport, animals, war. Sexual double standard: more partners accepted for boys, opposite for girls.
Silencing girls
Girls taught vague terms; abstinence-based education makes girls gatekeepers; marriage as only place for sex; sexuality seen as men's dangerous urges.
FGM
28+ African countries plus parts of Asia, Middle East. Clitoridectomy (clitoris removed), excision (plus inner lips), infibulation (plus outer lips sewn closed).
Making a difference
Safer sex needs information, motivation, skills; avoid generic appeals. Sexual subjectivity: aware of desires, entitled to pleasure, able to stand up for safety and pleasure (Schick). Feminists report the most positive sexual attitudes (342 college women).

Self-check

Q: Name the 3 parts of sexual subjectivity.
Awareness of desire; belief in entitlement to pleasure; ability to protect safety and pleasure.

Q: Excision vs infibulation?
Excision removes clitoris plus inner lips; infibulation adds sewing the outer lips closed.

Chapter 11The Second Half: Midlife and Aging

Age is social: stereotypes, bodies, relationships, work, and poverty.

Age cohort / Double standard of aging
Born in about the same decade / women are 'old' earlier than men (when they can't attract men or reproduce vs men when incapacitated).
Ageism
Prejudice and discrimination by age; can be internalized (depression). White American individualism: 'the old are bound to fail'; needing help seen as shameful. Interdependent cultures differ.
Self-identity vs social identity
Subjective feeling of age vs how age looks to others. Öberg (Sweden): most people feel and want to be younger; dissociating from age dissociates from the self.
Media
Older women least visible; sexuality most invisible; LGBTQ doubly invisible. Positive stereotypes tie to warmth, negative to competence. Kindly grandmother, mother-in-law from hell, little old lady. Elderspeak = baby-talk, infantilizing; stereotypes can affect medical care.
Health
Women live longer but have more chronic illness/disability (limits 2-3x more common over 75). Heart disease: male-based research; women misdiagnosed (panic disorder, menopause, hypochondria). Hispanic and African American women have poorer health; SES is the single best predictor of health in old age.
Menopause
End of periods, 40-60, average ~50. Hot flashes, vaginal thinning/dryness; not universal. Depression is not a normal part of menopause; possible short-term moodiness (hormones, physical changes, stereotypes). Possible relief. Exercise helps.
ERT / HRT
Reproductive hormones for symptoms and aging. HRT: slight increase in breast cancer, heart attack, stroke. ERT: stroke risk, no heart protection. Both linked to cognitive impairment. May benefit severe symptoms and osteoporosis in high-risk women.
Body project
Masquerading as younger; aggressive anti-aging marketing; some opt out. Exercise: prevents falls and disability, better mood and self-esteem; women exercise less than men and less with age; programs need social opportunity.
Sexuality
Older women deemed deviant for appearing sexual; Viagra jokes vs negativity about older women. Biggest factor: lack of partner (not enough men). 'Cougar' script. Older lesbians: ~60% in a relationship; friends vital.
Friends, family, grandmothers
Women have more and closer friends. Grandmother: one of few positive images; Native American grandmother as family center; African American multigenerational families. 4.9 million children in grandparent-headed homes, 2.5 million grandparents with full custody; over 57% of grandmother-alone households poor.
Caregiving
Most caregivers are women (parents, spouse). Costs (Cox): 33% cut hours, 29% passed up advancement, 22% took leave, 13% early retirement; lower Social Security. Not simply free choice: gender division of labor, devalued unpaid work, lack of social services.
Widowhood
Nearly half of women over 65 are widows; 27% met PTSD criteria early. Women cope better than men (expect widowhood, men lose a caregiver). Lesbians face heterosexism, hidden grief, legal/financial obstacles.
Work and retirement
College-educated women's early 50s: confidence, highest life satisfaction. Poor and working-class women: health problems sooner; slowing down is the goal. Retirement model fits women poorly (off-the-books work, unpaid workload continues, divorced/never married work longer). Two-thirds of poor older adults are women; caregiving years enter a zero in Social Security unless 35+ years of paid work.
Making a difference
Elder activism (often ignores women's issues; Baby Boomers); women's communal living (family of choice); resist ageism, even 'positive' stereotypes.

Self-check

Q: Best predictor of health in old age?
Socioeconomic status.

Q: Why does widowhood hit women differently?
Women expect it and cope better; men lose a caregiver.

Chapter 14Making a Difference: Toward a Better Future

Waves, backlash, feminist identity, and what students can do.

Waves
First: suffrage, early 1900s. Second: 1960s, reproductive rights, workplace equality, media sexism, nonsexist child raising, science and politics, ending violence. Third: continued objectification, high-tech reproductive rights, global trafficking, LGBTQ rights. (Review sheet says four waves; confirm the fourth in your text.)
Core values
Respect for differences is the cornerstone; a feminist values women's worth. Collective action (group solidarity) separates a movement from individual success.
Backlash (Faludi, 1991)
Attempts to put women, esp. feminists, back in their place; labeling feminists crazy, lesbian, man-hater.
Images of feminists
Positive: women working together. Negative: man-hating extremists ('feminazi'). Even people who don't believe it think others do. Feminists show less hostility toward men than non-feminists.
Attitudes and identity
Labeling brings stigma; many women reluctant. Women who label themselves feminists are more likely to be activists than like-minded women who don't (Yoder). Feminists reject unrealistic ideals; better well-being.
Impact on psychology
Women now earn most higher degrees in psychology; women and minorities lead organizations. Field named androcentrism and sexism and opened new topics.
Imagining equality
No gender-based violence, husbands do half the housework, children protected, no old woman in poverty, half of CEOs/judges/generals/legislators women.
What one student can do
Write papers on women/gender, independent study, question exclusion in class, join the women's center, NOW or Feminist Majority Foundation, volunteer at rape crisis hotline or shelter, help older women/single moms.
Grad school and work
Check women faculty and tenure; check gender and family policies of employers; keep educating yourself.

Self-check

Q: What distinguishes a movement from individual success?
Collective action.

Q: What predicts activism?
Identifying as a feminist, not just holding equality beliefs.
